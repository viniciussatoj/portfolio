<SectionTitle bar>Problem_</SectionTitle>

Liv Up built its reputation on curated, healthy frozen meals. During COVID we added traditional groceries, and the catalog grew from about 100 items to over 400. The new items were well reviewed. They just weren't being bought enough.

From previous data we knew that users who bought a greater variety of items had significantly higher retention. So low variety was two problems at once: missing revenue and an early churn signal.

<SectionTitle bar>Discovery_</SectionTitle>

Every week the squad met for Café com Números: a review of the KPIs, what was working, and whether any recent delivery had moved the numbers. It is where I learned to bring data into my design decisions, so I started the same way here. With the PM and our data analyst, I traced the path from opening the app to a completed order, before and after groceries launched.

New customers were buying groceries without trouble. Customers who had been active for six months or more were not. They repeated the same items month after month, and when they did try something different, it came from categories they already knew. In orders of 12+ items, more than half of the items had been bought before. Their habits crystallized after the second or third purchase.

The app gave them no reason to look. The home screen was a promotional banner, which about 30% of users opened, and category navigation, which only helps people who already know what they want.

<SectionTitle bar>Interviews_</SectionTitle>

I interviewed six repeat buyers, all active for six months or more, to understand how they decided what to buy and how they used the app. They knew where everything was. They had a fixed order, usually proteins, then sides, then dessert, and went straight to a category or to search. Some copied their last order, changed a few items and called it a day.

That was exactly what Liv Up had promised them: get in, buy, get out. A homepage full of hooks would have fought that, so the hypothesis went the other way. Make the fastest way to reorder also the place where new products show up. If it's the quickest route through the app, nobody has a reason to skip it.

<Fragment slot="aside">
<Findings n="6" unit="interviews" who="Repeat buyers active for 6+ months" heading="Insights">

- They knew exactly where to find what they were looking for.
- They had a set buying order. For example: proteins, sides and finally desserts.
- They didn't have the habit of exploring the app. New additions were found on their usual route or not found at all.

<SectionTitle bar>Results_</SectionTitle>

<BigNumbers

Through A/B testing, users with the shopping list enabled bought a **20% greater variety of items**, the metric we were designing for.

Over the following six months, that variety showed up in the basket: users who adopted the shopping list had a **13% higher average ticket**. On purchases with a completed order, sessions ran **2 minutes longer** on average, much of it spent on the shopping list page.

The feature was designed while Liv Up was moving to a new brand, which is why the work exists in two UI versions.
