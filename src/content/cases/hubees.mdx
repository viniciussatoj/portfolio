<SectionTitle bar>Problem_</SectionTitle>

Hubees is a subscription-based parking startup with first-mover advantage but our design infrastructure was quietly working against that.

Designers were redrawing the same patterns sprint after sprint. Developers received deliverables with inconsistent spacing, conflicting components, and small variations that were unclear they were intentional or not. Every handoff required a conversation that shouldn’t have been necessary.

Then a new brand identity landed on the roadmap. **The rebrand gave us the opportunity to fix the foundation and came with a deadline.**

<PullQuote>
  The real question was how much to lock down,
  <strong>enough structure to speed us up, not so much that it became a ceiling.</strong>
</PullQuote>

<SectionTitle bar>Foundation_</SectionTitle>

<Numbered heading="Auditing what already existed">

  I inventoried every component across every product, categorised by use case, frequency, and context. What emerged was a map of duplication, dozens of components doing the same job in slightly different ways.
  
  The first major decision was collapsing those variations: fewer components, each with a clearer semantic purpose.

  Color, typography, spacing, shadows and opacity were anchored to the new brand studies but defined with room to evolve.

  I've worked inside design systems that were locked down too early and became too much of a constraint rather than a tool. Systems that are over-specified at launch don't survive contact with real product decisions.
</Numbered>

<Numbered heading="Designing journeys first, components second">
  To pressure-test the system against real problems, I flipped the usual order. Instead of designing components and applying them to journeys, I designed each journey as if the design system didn't exist. Focused entirely on the best solution for the user.

  Only then did I work backwards to build components that could support those decisions and simplify where needed. It kept the system grounded in real product work keeping us from chasing hypothetical flexibility.

  <Figure src={journeys} wide alt="Journey maps for Registration, Stay Check-in and Stay Check-out" />

  We scoped the initial launch to the three highest-priority journeys — User Registration, Stay Check-in, and Stay Check-out — and timed it to coincide with the brand rollout.
</Numbered>
<SectionTitle bar>Results_</SectionTitle>
  Six months from kickoff to production launch, delivered in parallel with the rebrand campaign.

  Beyond the QA time reduction, the system changed how the team worked. Documented use cases made component decisions self-explanatory — in many cases the alignment conversation became unnecessary altogether. And with clear guidance on when and how to use the system, developers started prototyping improvements independently, validating direction before pulling anyone else in. That last part wasn't a goal we planned for. It emerged from the system being trustworthy enough to build on.
