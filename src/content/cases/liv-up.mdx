<SectionTitle bar>Problem_</SectionTitle>

Liv Up built its reputation on curated, healthy frozen meals. During COVID we identified a demand to include traditional grocery items, but they weren't selling as much as internal research suggested they would.

When they were bought, the new items were well-reviewed — but they weren't being bought enough.

From previous data we knew that users who purchased a greater variety of items had significantly higher retention rates. So low variety was both a revenue problem and a churn signal.

<SectionTitle bar>Discovery_</SectionTitle>

With help from our data analyst first I looked into who was buying the new products.

New customers were buying them just fine. But when we looked into our users who had been active for six months or more, we noticed a high rate of repeat purchases: the same items, month after month.
Moreover, when they did buy different items, these tended to come from categories similar to those they had already purchased. 

After a few months, their purchasing preferences seemed to have crystallized.



<Aside>

<SectionTitle bar>Interviews_</SectionTitle>

I ran interviews with repeat purchasers looking for patterns across users with similar purchase habits. My goal here was to find out how users decided what to buy and how they use our app to find their items. 

What all of them had in common is: once they knew what items they liked they stopped navigating through our catalog to discover new items. They knew where their favorite items where so they went straight to their respective categories or searched directly for them on our search bar.
Some of them even just copy pasted items from their last order, made a few adjustments and called it a day.

So we thought, when do we present new products to repeat buyers without feeling intrusive or complicating their shopping and checking out routine to the point of raising churn rates?

<Fragment slot="aside">
<Findings n="6" unit="interviews" who="Repeat buyers active for 6+ months" heading="Insights">

- They knew exactly where to find what they were looking for.
- They had a set buying order. For example: Proteins, sides and finally desserts
- They didn’t had the habit of exploring the app. New additions were found on their usual route or not found at all.

</Findings>
</Fragment>

</Aside>

<SectionTitle bar>Results_</SectionTitle>

Through A/B testing, users with the shopping list enabled bought a **20% greater variety of items**, the metric we were designing for.

Over the following six months, that variety showed up in the basket: users who adopted the shopping list had a **13% higher average ticket**. On purchases with a completed order, sessions ran **2 minutes longer** on average, much of it spent on the shopping list page.
